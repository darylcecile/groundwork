# Research behind the explanation skill

Most sources provide editorial guidance; the studies concern perceived
understanding and ease of processing. The skill adapts both to conversational
technical explanations.

## 1. Borrow STE principles within their scope

The official [ASD-STE100 overview](https://www.asd-ste100.org/about_STE.html) and
[FAQ](https://www.asd-ste100.org/STE_faq.html) describe a controlled language for
procedural and descriptive maintenance documentation. The skill borrows its
focused sentences, clear actors, and consistent terminology while retaining useful
technical vocabulary. Only the overview and FAQ were reviewed, not the full
Issue 9 rules and dictionary; STE is an influence rather than a claim of formal
conformance.

## 2. Explain the knowledge the reader is missing

[Google's audience guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/audience)
frames useful documentation around the gap between what readers know and what
they need to know. [Diataxis on explanation](https://diataxis.fr/explanation/)
emphasizes connections, context, causes, and consequences within a bounded topic.
Together they inform the skill's focus on the reader's missing connection and the
mechanism behind an outcome.

## 3. Make terminology useful

Google's guidance on [words](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/one/words)
and [jargon](https://developers.google.com/style/jargon) supports consistent names
and definitions suited to the audience. The W3C's supplemental
[clear-words guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o3p01-clear-words/)
also favors common words and explanations of unfamiliar language. These sources
inform the skill's balance of familiar wording and useful technical terms.

In an online experiment,
[Shulman and Bullock (2020)](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0239524)
found that jargon reduced reported ease of processing in policy and flood-risk
messages; its effect was not statistically significant in COVID-19 messages.
Topic familiarity and sampling limit how broadly these findings apply.

## 4. Sound like a helpful colleague

[Google's voice and tone guidance](https://developers.google.com/style/tone)
recommends a friendly, respectful tone without filler or claims that a task is
easy. [Microsoft's simple and human guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/brand-voice-above-all-simple-human)
supports leading with the important point and using everyday words and natural
contractions. Both inform the skill's colleague-like voice and removal of empty
prose.

## 5. Give examples an explanatory job

[Google's sample-code guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/sample-code)
prioritizes correct, understandable examples with useful explanations. Its
[illustration guidance](https://developers.google.com/tech-writing/two/illustrations)
emphasizes a clear instructional takeaway without distracting detail. The W3C's
supplemental [literal-language guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG2/supplemental/patterns/o3p04-literal-language/)
recommends concrete language and explaining metaphors and similes. The skill
adapts this guidance into examples that show a change and its outcome, and
analogies whose connections and consequential limits are explicit.

## 6. Check the mechanism behind a fluent answer

[Rozenblit and Keil (2002), *The misunderstood limits of folk science: an illusion
of explanatory depth*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3062901/)
found that participants often rated their understanding lower after attempting
detailed causal explanations of familiar devices. Independent ratings also
indicated that their initial self-assessments overstated understanding. The
opening and studies 1–6 informed this note. The skill's silent mechanism check is
an editorial application, not a method tested in the study.
